Rural Sites, Unpredictable Crews: Solving Accommodation for Archaeology and Heritage Projects

Digs run on a different clock: small teams, shifting dates and the nearest hotel miles away. Why standard booking breaks down on heritage fieldwork.

Introduction

A rural Norfolk project turns up something worth a second look, and the accommodation booked for 3 weeks now needs to stretch to 6. The team, which started at 12, needs 5 more hands for the extension. The nearest town with any hotel stock sits 40 minutes down single-track roads, and whoever booked the original accommodation now has to explain, apologetically, why the rooms they secured no longer cover the dates or the headcount.

This is a normal week in commercial archaeology, and it is exactly the scenario that trips up most accommodation providers.

Key findings

  • Standard accommodation suppliers are built around fixed locations, fixed headcounts and fixed dates, all 3 of which shift constantly on archaeological projects.
  • Self-catering, flexible booking terms and upfront pricing matter more in archaeology than in most sectors, because tight, often grant-funded budgets leave little room for surprise charges or cancellation fees.
  • A fit-for-purpose approach mobilises projects faster, since accommodation is arranged before the team needs an answer rather than scrambled together once a crew is already on site.

Why standard booking breaks down on archaeology projects

Ask a mainstream accommodation supplier to house a fieldwork team and the instinct is to reach for the nearest Travelodge. That instinct collides with the structure of archaeological fieldwork itself. Watching briefs and evaluations are typically secured as pre-commencement planning conditions, which is why sites sit wherever a development is happening rather than wherever accommodation is convenient, often landing teams in villages and farmland far from any chain hotel. Team sizes move as the work demands, growing when a trench needs extra hands and shrinking again once a phase closes. Weather delays and unexpected finds push timelines past their original dates without warning, turning a fixed booking into a liability the moment conditions change. Because larger projects often run on National Lottery Heritage Fund grants that can span a 2-year development phase and a 5-year delivery phase, self-catering accommodation is often the only way to keep costs inside the budget across that stretch of time.

Consumer booking platforms and a patchwork of local hotels can absorb 1 or 2 of these constraints at a time. Stack all of them across several concurrent sites, and someone on the project team ends up losing entire afternoons to phone calls and rebookings.

What that admin costs

Managing rural fieldwork through direct hotel bookings and consumer sites means standards vary from site to site, and the admin piles up on top of the archaeological work itself.

What a fit-for-purpose approach looks like

Solving this properly means designing around archaeology’s constraints from the outset. In practice that means a few things holding true regardless of where a site lands or how long the project runs.

Coverage needs to reach the rural stretches where accommodation is actually needed, since that is where excavation teams are based for weeks or months at a time. Self-catering needs to be the standard offer, built into the pricing from the start, because budget management on these projects depends on it. Booking terms have to flex when weather delays a phase or a find extends a project, with a phone call to an account manager settling the change on the spot. Pricing needs to be upfront enough that a project manager can plan against it from the funding application onward, with costs holding steady when a timeline moves. And whoever runs the account needs enough familiarity with the sector, including the professional standards CIfA sets for fieldwork practice, that watching briefs and community accommodation logistics only need explaining once.

The bigger picture

Archaeology and heritage work already carries enough variables: what the ground reveals, what the weather does, what the funding cycle allows. Accommodation is one variable a project manager should be able to take off that list. When a provider understands the shape of the sector, from initial evaluation through excavation to post-excavation work near the archives, project teams get to spend their time on the excavation, with the accommodation already sorted.

What this means for you

  • "Contractor accommodation" is not one problem. A rail night shift, an offshore rotation and a six-week dig need different briefs.
  • Start with the site, the start date and how many of you. The sector decides the rest of the questions.
  • If your sector is not here, ask us. The questions stay the same: distance, length of stay and notice.
