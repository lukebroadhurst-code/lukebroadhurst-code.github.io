What Net Zero Commitments Mean for Temporary Site Accommodation

Temporary accommodation is entering the scope 3 calculation. What net zero commitments mean for how you source it, and what to ask before the question lands.

In 2023, a quantity surveyor at a major civil engineering contractor received a new pre-qualification questionnaire for a government framework. The usual sections on financial standing and insurance were there. So was a new one: scope 3 emissions by category, with temporary accommodation listed separately for the first time.

The number sat nowhere in his records.

That moment is arriving for more contractors every month. As UK construction firms commit to science-based targets and net zero supply chains, every category of project spend is entering the carbon calculation, and temporary accommodation, long treated as a facilities cost with no sustainability dimension, is now firmly on the list.

Key findings

  • The built environment sector accounts for around 40% of the UK’s total carbon output.
  • Public sector procurement frameworks are increasingly requiring scope 3 disclosure by category as a pre-qualification condition, with accommodation falling within the business travel emissions that contractors must quantify.
  • Distance-to-site is the highest-impact carbon variable in accommodation procurement; workers travelling 25 miles each way on a 60-person project generate transport emissions that dwarf the savings from a cheaper nightly rate.
  • Energy performance varies significantly across the available property stock; the difference between an EPC D-rated property running gas heating and an EPC B-rated property with heat pump heating is material at scale.
  • Many of the UK’s largest contractors have science-based targets or Construction Leadership Council Route to Zero commitments that require scope 3 supply chain reductions.

Why accommodation has entered the net zero frame

The built environment sector accounts for around 40% of the UK’s total carbon output. Most of the industry’s attention has focused on operational carbon in permanent buildings and embodied carbon in materials, where the numbers are largest and the frameworks most developed. Temporary site accommodation has attracted almost no dedicated carbon analysis by comparison, and is treated as a short-term operational cost rather than a variable worth measuring.

The gap is closing, driven by 3 converging forces.

The first is public sector procurement. Government contracts governed by PPN 006, HS2’s supply chain requirements, and the Government Commercial Agency’s (formerly Crown Commercial Service) net zero obligations require contractors bidding on contracts over £5m to produce a Carbon Reduction Plan disclosing Scope 3 emissions across five mandated categories. Temporary workforce accommodation falls within the business travel emissions that contractors are increasingly expected to quantify, and as Scope 3 reporting is set to expand to all 15 categories for NHS suppliers from 2027, with wider public sector frameworks expected to follow, it will come into sharper focus. A contractor who cannot produce project-level carbon data broken down by category faces a growing disadvantage in pre-qualification scoring for public sector work, which accounts for the majority of long-duration infrastructure projects in the UK.

The second is corporate science-based targets. Many of the UK’s largest contractors have SBTi commitments or Construction Leadership Council Route to Zero pledges that require reductions in scope 3 emissions across their supply chains. When a sustainability team models the full scope 3 footprint, accommodation appears under multiple categories at once: energy consumption in occupied buildings, transport from accommodation to site, and in some reporting frameworks, the embodied carbon of temporary structures.

The third is pressure from client ESG reporting. Major infrastructure clients are reporting to their own investors and government stakeholders on the carbon performance of their programmes, and project-level accommodation data features in those audits alongside plant, materials, and transport. Disclosure is becoming a standard expectation at handover, not an optional addendum.

What the accommodation carbon footprint actually looks like

The carbon variables in site accommodation procurement are straightforward once they are mapped.

Distance from site is the highest-impact factor. Workers travelling 25 miles each way from a budget hotel, because near-site options were either unavailable or left unprocured, generate transport emissions that exceed any savings from the cheaper nightly rate. On a 60-person project running 18 months, the difference between a well-sourced near-site portfolio and a scattered one can run to tens of tonnes of CO2. This is where accommodation management matters most: a provider with genuine national coverage, searching within the right radius from day one, removes this variable before it compounds.

Energy performance of the property follows. An EPC D-rated guesthouse running gas central heating generates significantly more emissions per occupied night than an EPC B-rated property with heat pump heating. This difference becomes material across the full portfolio on projects with large accommodation footprints. The minimum energy efficiency standards for domestic properties in England and Wales are tightening, which will gradually raise the floor of the available market. Still, the spread across the current stock remains wide.

Catering and waste profiles complete the picture. Properties with communal catering generate different waste and energy profiles than those where workers self-cater, a distinction that sustainability-conscious clients are beginning to include in project carbon assessments.

What net zero-compliant accommodation procurement looks like

Contractors building this capability are treating accommodation as a sustainability decision from the point of mobilisation. In practice, that means working with providers who report on energy performance ratings, distance-to-site for each property, heating fuel type, and waste management arrangements, and who hold that data at project level so it feeds directly into scope 3 reporting at handover.

The distinction that matters for construction and infrastructure clients is not simply whether a provider offers carbon reporting, hotel booking platforms have begun introducing this for conventional business travel, but whether the carbon data is built around a managed residential property portfolio, sourced near project sites, and reported at project level in a format ready for scope 3 disclosure at handover. That is a different proposition from a hotel search tool, and it is the one that serves the pre-qualification requirements that contractors are now encountering on public sector frameworks.

The scale of the gap in the wider market is significant. According to the most recent industry research, only 5% of serviced accommodation operators are currently tracking Scope 3 emissions, and fewer than a quarter have calculated even their most basic Scope 1 emissions. Most platforms that offer carbon reporting focus on hotel stays for conventional corporate travel, not on the managed near-site residential portfolios required by construction and infrastructure programmes.

Providers who can supply verified carbon data, offer a portfolio with genuine near-site coverage, and maintain consistent energy performance ratings across their stock are becoming the default choice for public sector programmes where carbon reporting is a contractual requirement. Providers who supply accommodation without this data leave the contractor with an incomplete scope 3 disclosure that clients and framework auditors will catch at handover.

The split that is already opening

Several of the UK’s largest contractors are now running accommodation procurement through their sustainability teams alongside commercial teams, because the carbon data requirement means someone with ESG responsibility must approve supplier selection. That is a structural change in how accommodation decisions are made, and it is moving faster in the public sector supply chain than anywhere else.

For contractors still treating accommodation as a pure cost category, the adjustment is coming from 2 directions at once: from clients who will ask for carbon data at handover, and from their own sustainability teams who need it for annual Scope 3 reporting. Building that reporting capability retrospectively, from invoices and property addresses after the project has ended, is considerably harder than capturing it at source during procurement.

As public sector framework requirements tighten and Scope 3 reporting becomes standard on major infrastructure programmes over the next 3 years, accommodation providers that have built audit-ready carbon reporting into their core proposition will find their position on approved supplier lists increasingly secure.

What this means for you

  • Temporary accommodation is turning up in scope 3 questions. Know where your data will come from before an auditor asks.
  • Start with distance to site. It is the easiest variable to measure.
  • Ask for what you need to report: postcodes, nights and property type, in a form you can hand to an auditor.
