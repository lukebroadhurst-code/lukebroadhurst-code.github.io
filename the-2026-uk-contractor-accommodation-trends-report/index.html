The 2026 UK Contractor Accommodation Trends Report

Demand, the limits of hotels, serviced apartments, tighter short-let rules and ESG in every tender. The trends shaping where UK contractor crews stay in 2026.

1 June 2026 · 7 min read

By the Rooost team ·Updated 8 September 2026

On any given Wednesday morning this spring, roughly 30,000 people clock in to work on HS2, another 14,000 between Hinkley Point C and Sizewell C, and 30,000 more across the offshore wind sector. Almost none of them sleep in their own bed that night. This report sets out the trends shaping where they stay, who books it, and what it costs in 2026.

Key findings

  • Demand is surging. 47,860 extra UK construction workers needed each year through 2029.
  • Hotels stop working past 2 weeks. London ADR forecast at £193.50 for 2026.
  • Serviced apartments are doubling. £6.52bn UK market by 2033, 8.8% CAGR.
  • Book earlier. 28% of stays now reserved 60+ days out, up from 25%.
  • Airbnb sourcing is risky. Mandatory STL registration; £5,000 penalty for non-compliance.
  • ESG is in every tender. CSRD makes Scope 3 emissions reporting compulsory.

The pipeline has shifted into a different gear

CITB’s Construction Skills Network forecasts that the UK construction sector will need an additional 47,860 workers each year between 2025 and 2029, a cumulative requirement of around 239,300 people, simply to keep pace with current activity. Layer on the government’s commitment to deliver 1.5 million new homes by 2029, the £100bn+ of energy infrastructure in the pipeline, and the scale-up of gigafactories and data centres, and the headline figure rises further. CITB warns in its Industry Picture 2026 report that the widening skills gap risks tangible societal consequences if left unaddressed.

The pressure is most visible on a handful of major projects. HS2 now employs around 30,000 people, having recently passed the milestone and exceeded its target of 2,000 apprenticeships before construction was finished. Hinkley Point C employs 12,000 on-site, with a further 3,000 expected through the next year and a peak workforce of 26,000 reached in May 2025. At Sizewell C, more than 2,000 people are working daily as the project enters a more intensive construction phase, building towards a peak of about 8,000. RenewableUK forecasts that offshore wind, which employs over 30,000 today, will require 88,509 jobs by the end of 2026 and more than 100,000 by 2030 if the UK is to hit its 50GW target.

Each thousand workers added to a project translates into roughly 800 to 900 beds needed within a commutable radius, since contractors typically rotate on patterns that bring most of the workforce on-site at the same time. The arithmetic gets sharper on rural sites, where the available housing stock within a 45-minute drive can often be measured in hundreds rather than thousands.

Hotels are losing the long-stay battle

For projects running longer than 2 weeks, the economics of a hotel room break down quickly. PwC has forecast London hotel ADR at £193.50 for 2026, with national rates climbing from £189.00 in 2024 to £191.55 in 2025. VisitBritain data shows the England ADR up 3% year-on-year in early 2026, reaching £146.

The cost gap matters because contractor projects are getting longer, not shorter. A wind farm commissioning rotation might run 6 to 8 weeks. A nuclear decommissioning placement can stretch beyond a year. At £140 to £200 per night across hotel inventory, with no kitchen, no laundry, and a 1pm checkout that conflicts with shift patterns, the numbers stop adding up well before week 3.

What’s replacing the hotel night is a portfolio of options: serviced apartments where the budget allows, mid-let HMOs where it doesn’t, longer-term residential rentals on multi-year projects, and a growing category of purpose-built workforce accommodation near rural infrastructure sites. The procurement question has shifted from “which hotel do we book?” to “which mix of inventory matches this project’s rotation, location and budget?”

The serviced apartment sector is doubling

Savills’ 2026 European Serviced Apartment Report puts the UK extended-stay market on track to double in value to £6.52 billion by 2033, growing at a compound annual rate of 8.8%. Investor appetite, measured in the firm’s annual Operational Real Estate Survey, rose 22% in 2026 alone. Energy, technology, legal and finance are the sectors driving the strongest demand for relocation and project-based stays.

For procurement teams, the practical implication is that supply is expanding where it’s needed most. New aparthotel openings are clustering around regional infrastructure corridors, data centre hubs in the East of England, and the energy belt running from Somerset up through Suffolk and into the North Sea coast. Where 5 years ago a long-stay booking near a remote site meant a portacabin or a 90-minute commute from the nearest town, the answer in 2026 is increasingly a 1-bedroom or 2-bedroom serviced apartment booked through a managed-accommodation provider.

Booking behaviour is changing in step. In the first half of 2025, 28% of UK serviced apartment reservations were made more than 60 days in advance, up from 25% in H1 2024. That 3-point shift looks small in isolation, although it reflects a structural change, since project teams have learned that the best-located stock disappears early, and the cost of last-minute booking now justifies forward planning that didn’t happen a few years ago.

Regulation has redrawn the supply map

Anyone who relied informally on Airbnb stock for contractor placements through the early 2020s has had a sharp lesson in regulatory exposure. The national short-term let registration scheme for England, enabled by the Levelling Up and Regeneration Act 2023, launched in 2025. By 2026, anyone operating accommodation provided for fewer than 90 consecutive nights for payment must register with their local authority, hold a registration number, and meet fire safety, gas safety and insurance requirements. Platforms are now prohibited from listing unregistered properties, with civil penalties of up to £5,000 for non-compliance.

Sitting alongside the register is the C5 planning use class, introduced in 2024, which separates short-term lets from standard dwellinghouse use (C3) once a property crosses the 90-night threshold. London continues to operate its 90-day rule under the Deregulation Act 2015. Scotland has had mandatory licensing since 2022. Wales is rolling out registration and a visitor levy through 2026 and 2027.

For Rooost and other managed-accommodation providers, the compliance machinery has become a competitive moat. Keeping a portfolio compliant across 4 jurisdictions, multiple property types and constantly shifting local rules now demands operational depth that few suppliers can match. For clients, the practical consequence is that informal sourcing through consumer platforms carries reputational and contractual risk that a managed supplier absorbs.

ESG and consolidation are now procurement questions

The Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD) requires eligible companies to disclose Scope 3 emissions, which include business travel and accommodation. The European Sustainability Reporting Standard E1 mandates disclosure of methodology, data quality and target-setting for those emissions. UK companies in scope of CSRD through their European operations are already restructuring their procurement processes to capture the data.

The downstream effect on contractor accommodation is visible in every major tender. Sustainability clauses now sit alongside price and availability, with Green Key or equivalent certifications routinely required, EV charging asked about for sites with on-site parking, and CO2 reporting expected per booking. Investment-grade clients want a single emissions figure per project rather than a spreadsheet of hotel invoices, and they want it produced by their supplier rather than reconstructed by their finance team 6 months after the project closes.

Finance directors are pushing hard to consolidate at the same time, since a typical multi-site project can generate accommodation invoices from 40 or 50 separate hotels, holiday-let landlords and serviced apartment operators across the year. A single supplier, a single invoice, and standardised data make CSRD reporting workable, expense reconciliation faster, and procurement audits less painful. When accommodation is sourced through a managed provider, contractors arrive to a known standard whether they’re in Suffolk or Sutherland.

What it means for 2026

The UK has not built infrastructure at this pace, with this geographic spread and this dependence on mobile labour, since the 1960s motorway programme. For project teams, four working assumptions hold for 2026.

The supply-demand balance favours suppliers near the major projects. Stock near HS2 stations, Hinkley, Sizewell, the offshore wind corridor, the East of England data-centre cluster and the M62 industrial belt will be tighter and more expensive than the equivalent inventory in a quieter region. Booking 8 to 12 weeks ahead now produces materially better results than booking 2 weeks ahead, and the gap is widening.

Hotels remain useful for stays under 2 weeks. Beyond that point, serviced apartments and managed contractor housing produce better cost-per-night, better workforce experience and easier compliance reporting. The breakeven point falls earlier on rural sites, where hotel pricing is often inflated by a single dominant operator.

Compliance has become a procurement criterion in its own right. Any provider asked for proof of registration, planning class, fire and gas safety, insurance, and emissions methodology should produce it on request; those that hesitate carry project risk that outweighs any saving.

Consolidation pays back faster than it used to. The administrative overhead of managing 40 accommodation suppliers across a project has risen sharply with CSRD-style reporting requirements, and the time savings from a single managed-supplier relationship are real and quantifiable, particularly for finance and ESG teams whose attention is the binding constraint.

What this means for you

  • Treat accommodation as something planned alongside labour, not after it.
  • Check how much hotel stock sits near your site before you assume a hotel will do.
  • Put a number on your own crew rather than relying on a market average.
