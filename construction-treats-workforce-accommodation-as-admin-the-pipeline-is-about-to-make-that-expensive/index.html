Construction Treats Workforce Accommodation as Admin. The Pipeline Is About to Make That Expensive

The infrastructure pipeline is huge, yet accommodation still runs on credit cards and spreadsheets. What offshore learned years ago that construction has not.

21 April 2026 · 3 min read

By the Rooost team ·Updated 5 May 2026

  5. Sources

The UK’s infrastructure pipeline is the largest in a generation. The workforce it requires will need somewhere to sleep, and construction has never had a formal answer for how that gets managed, or who is responsible for managing it.

Key highlights

  • The UK infrastructure pipeline just hit £718bn. Somewhere in that number is a procurement problem nobody has formally assigned to anyone.
  • Accommodation treated as an afterthought looks familiar: rates negotiated at the last minute, workers placed miles from site, spend that appears in the finance report six weeks after it happened.
  • The offshore sector solved this twenty years ago. Construction is still largely working from the same credit card and spreadsheet approach it used on projects half this scale.
  • This is not a technology gap. It is a procurement maturity gap between how oil and gas manages workforce accommodation and how construction manages it.
  • At the scale the pipeline demands, the informal approach has a cost. It compounds across every project that runs it.

The pipeline and the problem it brings with it

In March 2026, the National Infrastructure and Service Transformation Authority published a construction pipeline valued at £718bn, covering projects scheduled across the next decade. The projects range from road and rail to energy and water, and they share a common feature that tends not to appear in the headline figures: they all require workers to be somewhere other than home, for extended periods, at scale.

Accommodation for that workforce, running across hundreds of concurrent projects, represents a procurement category that most construction businesses have never treated as a procurement category at all. It gets handled, usually by whoever has the time and the login for a booking site, and the cost lands in the accounts as a diffuse smear of hotel receipts and expense claims that no single person has ever totted up with any rigour.

What the energy sector worked out under pressure

Offshore oil and gas didn’t arrive at managed workforce accommodation through foresight. It arrived there because the alternative, ad hoc booking for workers on platforms 150 miles from the nearest hotel, was plainly unworkable. What followed was a discipline that construction has largely never had to develop: centralised procurement of accommodation, welfare standards tied to productivity and retention, and live visibility over occupancy and cost.

Camp managers on offshore installations know, on any given day, how many beds are occupied, what each one costs, and whether that number is tracking against the project budget. A procurement lead managing accommodation spend across a portfolio of construction projects is typically working from a spreadsheet assembled from credit card statements, with a lag of several weeks between the spend and the knowledge of it.

What procurement maturity looks like at pipeline scale

The construction companies that will absorb the largest share of a £718bn pipeline are, in several cases, already operating at a scale where informal accommodation management produces visible and quantifiable problems: duplicated bookings, rates that vary by £40 a night for identical rooms depending on who made the call, workers placed in accommodation with commute times that eat into rest periods, and a total cost per head that nobody in procurement has ever formally benchmarked.

The offshore model is not exotic. It requires a preferred supplier framework, a defined welfare standard, a person with accountability for the category, and a system that captures occupancy data in something closer to real time than month-end. None of that is complicated. What it does require is the decision to treat accommodation as a procurement responsibility rather than an administrative inconvenience, which is a different kind of decision from the ones the industry is used to making about it.

What this means for you

  • Plan accommodation alongside labour, not after it. Decide who owns it before you mobilise.
  • Agree what happens when dates move and who picks up the phone in the evening, before it matters.
  • Count how many invoices a project would generate if it ran five properties at once. That is the admin to design out.
